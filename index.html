The Cost of “Clean”: Lithium Mining, Indigenous Sovereignty, and Supply Chain Justice at Peehee Mu’huh

Green energy gets sold to us as clean and ethical. At Thacker Pass that label doesn’t hold up, because the lithium is coming out of land that was permitted for mining without the consent of the Indigenous people connected to it.

By Brayden Cunliffe · Thacker Pass, Humboldt County, Nevada

Thesis

Batteries, wind turbines, and solar storage get pushed as the clean and ethical future, but that is a massive misconception when the lithium behind them comes from a mine that was permitted without the free, prior, and informed consent of the Indigenous peoples connected to the land. I use Indigenous voices, the legal record, and engineering research to show why. I also point out where this story gets more complicated than one side being completely right.

Section 1 · Indigenous knowledge systems

Land that already has a name

Most maps and permits call this place Thacker Pass. Paiute and Shoshone people call it Peehee Mu’huh, which AP reporting translates as “rotten moon” [12]. The name comes from oral history. The tribes say that in 1865 Paiute hunters came home to find elders, women, and children killed by U.S. cavalry [12]. HRW reports that Tribal members still come back every year to commemorate the massacre and use the land for religious practice [4].

The People of Red Mountain (Atsa koodakuh wyh Nuwu) were formed by Daranda Hinkey, a granddaughter of Fort McDermitt Paiute and Shoshone citizen Elwood Hinkey, according to an ICT photo caption [5]. HRW quotes the group’s spokesperson, Gary McKinney, putting the mine inside a longer history of mining, broken treaties, and land damage on Paiute, Shoshone, and Bannock landscapes [4]. The group’s own site is source [1].

Odette Auger (Sagamok Anishnawbek) reports for Watershed Sentinel, in a piece republished by Intercontinental Cry, that McKinney is a descendant of one of three survivors of the 1865 massacre. He describes the area as important for hunting, trading, and plant harvesting, with natural gardens and springs that supply medicines. “Life was born there,” he says [13]. McKinney also says thousands of documented artifacts and cultural sites, including petroglyphs, were never raised in the litigation because he and others were shut out when they tried to intervene [13]. That is one person’s account and I haven’t seen it tested in court, but it points at the gap between what a legal record counts and what a community knows about its own land.

This is the part I want engineers to think about. Whether this land is “empty” depends on whose knowledge counts. An environmental review can measure acres, water, and wildlife. It has a much harder time measuring a place that a community returns to every year to remember the people they lost. That is knowledge too, and it’s why my definition of clean has to include it.

PERMITTING LENS

What the process counted

  • BLM approved the mine on January 15, 2021. The permit process took about a year, compared to a 3.1-year agency average [4].
  • BLM said it was in contact with tribal governments from early on. HRW and the ACLU found that this was three rounds of mailings to three tribal governments [4].
  • Lithium Americas says BLM permitted the project after years of government-to-government consultation [4].
RELATIONAL LENS

What the process struggled to see

  • Access matters for religious and cultural practice. Construction has blocked Numu/Nuwu and Newe people from parts of Peehee Mu’huh [4].
  • The area includes places where people fled and died in the 1865 massacre [4].
  • HRW and the ACLU found that at least five tribal governments opposed the project [4].

Section 2 · The legal record

The courts mostly sided with the government, and that is part of my argument

This is the part of the story that’s easy to skip, but the page is stronger with it. The Reno-Sparks Indian Colony and the Burns Paiute Tribe, along with the People of Red Mountain, challenged BLM’s approval in federal court [10]. In July 2023 the Ninth Circuit ruled that BLM did not violate federal environmental law when it approved the mine [11]. The court also found that BLM reasonably identified tribes for consultation, and it rejected the Burns Paiute Tribe’s claim that it should have been contacted [10].

In November 2023 a federal judge ruled against another round of tribal claims and noted that the tribes had not proved the project site is where the 1865 killings happened [12]. The government’s lawyers had argued that much of the evidence about the site’s sacred nature came after the decision was made [11].

So I can’t say the government was found to have broken the law. What I can say is that those cases were decided under U.S. statutes, while HRW and the ACLU measure the same permit against international human rights standards and say it falls short [4]. That gap is my whole argument. A legal process can be satisfied while consent never gets obtained.

Section 3 · Ecological and regional footprint

Who carries the cost

An engineering review from the Colorado School of Mines, written about lithium for electric aircraft, makes a point that applies to cars too. Countries driving demand for lithium can make social and environmental problems worse in mining countries while collecting the net environmental benefits of electrification [7]. The authors also say these local pressures get overlooked when climate targets are set [7].

Four places the cost shows up

HRW reports that the mine is not displacing any communities, but construction and a fence now restrict access to parts of Peehee Mu’huh that Indigenous people use for religious and cultural practice [4]. So the harm is losing access to a place, not being removed from homes. McKinney says company security have told his grandmother the road is closed, even though entry isn’t formally forbidden, and that she no longer feels able to go up and pray there [13].

Tribal residents told HRW they fear the mine threatens their rights to health, a healthy environment, and water [4]. Waste is going to be stored in a tailings stack about 350 feet high. Great Basin Resource Watch has warned it could fail catastrophically, while Lithium Americas says courts have repeatedly upheld the environmental analysis [4]. The Ninth Circuit found that BLM’s analysis reasonably discussed ways to reduce harm, including groundwater impacts [10]. These are contested risks, not proven contamination.

OPB reported in December 2025 that a mining company received federal approval to do exploratory drilling for lithium in southeast Oregon, which could eventually lead to a mine in some of the best sage grouse habitat left in the region [9]. This is exploration, not an operating mine. It matters because ranchers and conservationists spent about a decade on voluntary agreements to keep the bird off the endangered species list, and Oregon’s population has gone up but is still 33% below its historic level [9].

Chen-Glasser and DeCaluwe describe a split where the places that supply lithium take on the social and environmental pressure while the countries creating the demand get the net benefit [7]. For engineers that raises a design question. A product’s footprint doesn’t end at its spec sheet.

Section 4 · Human rights and international law

What free, prior, and informed consent asks for

HRW and the ACLU’s February 2025 report concluded that BLM permitted the mine without getting the free, prior, and informed consent of the Northern Paiute and Western Shoshone peoples, which violates their rights to religion, culture, and ancestral lands under international human rights law [4]. They also point to the 1872 Mining Law, which lets companies claim minerals on most federal land, much of it taken from Indigenous peoples, and say it helped set the Thacker Pass permit in motion [4].

Oliver Hailes, in the Journal of International Economic Law, goes through the international rules that govern the lithium industry. He argues that investment and trade treaties can be read in light of states’ obligations on human rights, environmental protection, and Indigenous peoples, which gives legal form to supply chain justice [6]. His case studies are Chile, Australia, Argentina, and China, not the United States, so I use him for the framework and not for Thacker Pass itself [6].

Free

No pressure or coercion. HRW and the ACLU document opposition from at least five tribal governments before the project moved ahead [4].

Prior

Consultation before the permit. The courts rebuffed tribal challenges to consultation that came after the agency had issued the permit [4].

Informed

Full information. The Burns Paiute Tribe says BLM’s consultation was “rife with withheld information” [12], but the Ninth Circuit rejected that claim [10].

Consent

A real ability to say no. HRW calls for halting construction until consent is obtained [4]. The summary of the May 2026 Native America Calling episode says an Amnesty International report found the government and mining companies are ignoring tribes’ FPIC rights in Nevada [3].

Section 5 · Sovereignty in action

The resistance is organized, legal, and public

Courts

Federal lawsuits

The Reno-Sparks Indian Colony, Burns Paiute Tribe, and People of Red Mountain were plaintiffs in the challenge to BLM’s approval [10]. Their legal arguments lost at the Ninth Circuit [11].

Public

Courthouse rally

On January 5, 2023, dozens of tribal citizens and other protesters beat drums and waved signs outside the federal courthouse in Reno during a hearing, according to AP’s Scott Sonner, republished by ICT [5].

Ceremony

Prayer rides and remembrance

HRW documents Tribal members commemorating the 1865 massacre every year and a 2024 prayer horse ride for Peehee Mu’huh [4].

Media

Indigenous radio

Native America Calling spent part of its May 21, 2026 episode on Nevada’s mining boom [3].

Section 6 · Where the story is not simple

Indigenous perspectives are not unanimous

HRW reports that the Fort McDermitt Paiute-Shoshone Tribe has since signed an agreement with Lithium Americas and sent a letter supporting the mine [4]. That doesn’t erase the opposition of other tribes and of the People of Red Mountain, who include descendants of Fort McDermitt people [5]. It does mean I can’t write “the tribes” like they’re one voice. HRW notes that at least six Tribes have a connection to the land at Thacker Pass [4], and McKinney says community benefit agreements have been reached with two tribes while arguing that the company’s approach divides the community [13]. To me this makes consent the real question: who gets to give it, and whether every group with ties to the land gets heard.

There is also a fair counterargument I haven’t fully answered. Climate change harms communities too, including Indigenous ones, and lithium is a key metal for batteries [4]. My answer is that urgency is a reason to build a better process, not a reason to skip consent. I would like to find more Indigenous voices who take on this tension directly.

Section 7 · Engineering ethics

What I think engineers owe the supply chain

Going back to my original question, does Paiute and Shoshone resistance change what clean and sustainable should mean? I think it does, with the complications above. If a supply chain can be legally permitted while the consent standard recognized internationally goes unmet, then legal and ethical are two different bars, and engineers should be clear about which one they’re clearing. The current method needs to change, otherwise we solve one problem by creating another.

Working conditions for a better supply chain

Consent before approval, with a real ability to say no [4]. Sourcing judged on rights and not only cost, which is the supply chain justice idea [6]. Attention to who gets the benefits and who takes on the burden [7]. And reform of the 1872 Mining Law, which HRW recommends [4]. These are conditions my sources support, not a full policy plan.
